	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10B 2014}) In the addition shown below $A, B, C,$ and $D$ are distinct digits. How many different values are possible for $D$?


 
 \[\begin{array}{lr}&ABBCB\\ +& BCADA\\ \hline & DBDDD\end{array}\]


 $\textbf{(A) }2\qquad\textbf{(B) }4\qquad\textbf{(C) }7\qquad\textbf{(D) }8\qquad\textbf{(E) }9$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }C



\textbf{Solution 1}

Note from the addition of the last digits that $A+B=D\text{ or }A+B=D+10$. From the addition of the frontmost digits, $A+B$ cannot have a carry, since the answer is still a five-digit number. Also $A + B$ cant have a carry since then for the second column, $C + 1 + D$ cant equal $D$. Therefore $A+B=D$. 

 Using the second or fourth column, this then implies that $C=0$, so that $B+C=B$ and $C+D=D$. Note that all of the remaining equalities are now satisfied: $A+B=D, B+C=B,$ and $B+A=D$. Since the digits must be distinct, the smallest possible value of $D$ is $1+2=3$, and the largest possible value is $9$. Thus we have that $3\le D\le9$, so the number of possible values is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) }7}$

 



\textbf{Solution 2 (Similar to 1, Less Math Jargon)}

Notice that $A$ and $B$ cannot equal $0$. 

 First, we can easily find that $C = 0$. Look at the middle column of the addition problem, which reads $B + C = B$. The only digit you can add to $B$ without changing its value is zero, so $C = 0$.

 Next, we can deduce that $A+B$ cannot be a two-digit number because it would create a carry. Look at the column immediately to the left of the middle one: $C + D = D$. Since $C = 0$, this is just $0 + D = D$. If the column to its right ($A + B$) generated a carry of $1$, this column would become $1 + 0 + D = D$, which is impossible ($1 + D \neq D$). Therefore, $A + B$ cannot carry over; it must be a single digit, meaning $A + B = D$.

 Since all digits must be distinct and $C = 0$, the smallest possible non-zero digits we can choose for $A$ and $B$ are $1$ and $2$. This means the minimum value of $A+B = D$ is $1+2=3$. Since $D$ must remain a single digit, the maximum value of $D$ is $9$. 

 Thus, there are $9 - 3 + 1$ values of $D$, which implies $\boxed{\textbf{(C) }7}$.

We multiply this by $100$ to get the percent value, and then round up because $n$ is the smallest integer that provides a greater discount than $28.75$, leaving us with the answer of $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 29}$

 



\textbf{Solution 2 (a bit easier)}

Assume the original price was $100$ dollars. Thus, after a discount of $n\%$, the price will be $100-n$ dollars. Using basic calculations, find out the value of the other discounts. This leaves us with the prices: $100-n$, $\frac{289}{4}$, $\frac{9^3}{10}$, and $\frac{15\cdot19}{4}$. Simplify these to get $100-n$, $72$ (rounded down), $72.9$, and $71$ (rounded down). 	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10B 2014}) In rectangle $ABCD$, $DC=2 \cdot CB$ and points $E$ and $F$ lie on $\overline{AB}$ so that $\overline{ED}$ and $\overline{FD}$ trisect $\angle ADC$ as shown. What is the ratio of the area of $\triangle DEF$ to the area of rectangle $ABCD$?

\textbf{Solution 2 (Complex Compliments)}

We can use a clever approach for this problem by finding out how many non-adjacency's exist and subtracting it by the total configurations.

 The formula \( C_n(A) = \frac{n}{k} \binom{n-k-1}{k-1} \) represents the Circular Gap Theorem, a crucial formula for finding out how many compliment cases exist where we want \( k \) values to not be adjacent from \( n \) total values.

 Considering reflections and rotations are practically the same, we see that we have \( (n-1)!/2 \), \( n=5 \) ways = \( 4!/2 = 24/2 = 12 \) total configurations.

 Now, we do some casework. Notice that we have \( n=5 \) values. Our \( k \in \{1,2,3,4,5\} \). We see that the values \( k \in \{3, 4, 5\} \) don't work as they make \( \binom{n-k-1}{k-1} \) invalid (3 gives \( 5-3-1 \choose 3-1 \) = \( 1 \choose 2 \) which doesn't exist, 4 gives \( 0 \choose 3 \) which doesn't exist, and 5 gives \( -1 \choose 4 \) which obviously doesn't exist).

 We solve for \( k \in \{1,2\} \). When \( k \in 1 \), we get \( C_n(A) = \frac{5}{1} \binom{5-1-1}{1-1} = 5(1) = 5 \) cases and when \( k \in 2 \), we get \( C_n(A) = \frac{5}{2} \binom{5-2-1}{2-1} = \frac{5}{2}(2) = 5 \). The total cases that lead to a good sum is \( 5+5=10 \).

 The answer isn't 10 however, because the theorem finds the compliments of the values, so the number of compliments is 10. We need to find the bad cases, which is \( 12 - 10 \) or $\textbf{(B)} \text{ }2$

 \textbf{Circular Gap Theorem Simplification} It is also helpful to note \( C_n(A) = \frac{n}{k} \binom{n-k-1}{k-1} \) = \( \binom{n}{2k} \).

\textbf{Solution 4 (Explaining the Martingale Shortcut Mentioned Previously)}

Instead of grinding through a massive system of 9 equations, we can solve this problem by assigning a "score value" to each lily pad. 

 Normally, if a game is perfectly fair (like a 50/50 coin flip), your average score after a turn is the same as your score before the turn. But this frog pond is rigged because the probabilities change at every pad. To fix this, we can mathematically design a custom scoring system that forces the game to be perfectly fair. 

 Once the scoring system is perfectly balanced, the law of expected value tells us that the probability of winning is just a simple ratio:
 \[\text{Escape Probability} = \frac{\text{Score of your Starting Pad}}{\text{Score of the Winning Pad (Pad 10)}}\]

 
                \begin{itemize}
                    \item (Note: We set the score of Pad 0 to be 0 points, so getting eaten by the snake gives 0 credit).*
                \end{itemize}


                \textbf{Step 1: Find the Step Rule} To keep our scores perfectly balanced at any pad $N$, the reward for a rare forward jump must perfectly offset the risk of an easy backward jump. This gives us a simple rule for how the point-gaps between pads must scale:

 
 \[\text{Step Multiplier} = \frac{\text{Probability of jumping Left}}{\text{Probability of jumping Right}} = \frac{\frac{N}{10}}{\frac{10-N}{10}} = \frac{N}{10-N}\]

 This fraction $\frac{N}{10-N}$ tells us exactly how much larger the next point-step must be to keep our scoring scale balanced.

 \textbf{Step 2: Build the Chain of Gaps} We start by declaring that moving from Pad 0 to Pad 1 awards exactly $1$ point. To find the point value of each subsequent step, we take our current step value and multiply it by our Step Multiplier rule:

 
                \begin{itemize}
                    \item \textbf{Step 1 (Pad 0 to 1):} Worth $1$ point. Pad 1 Score = $1$.\item \textbf{Step 2 (Pad 1 to 2):} Multiply by the rule at $N=1 \implies 1 \times \frac{1}{9} = \frac{1}{9}$ points.\item \textbf{Step 3 (Pad 2 to 3):} Multiply by the rule at $N=2 \implies \frac{1}{9} \times \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{36}$ points.\item \textbf{Step 4 (Pad 3 to 4):} Multiply by the rule at $N=3 \implies \frac{1}{36} \times \frac{3}{7} = \frac{1}{84}$ points.\item \textbf{Step 5 (Pad 4 to 5):} Multiply by the rule at $N=4 \implies \frac{1}{84} \times \frac{4}{6} = \frac{1}{126}$ points.
                \end{itemize}


                Because the pond's layout is perfectly symmetric, the point values for the remaining five steps mirror these numbers exactly in reverse order. This gives us all 10 step values:
 \[\left[ 1, \, \frac{1}{9}, \, \frac{1}{36}, \, \frac{1}{84}, \, \frac{1}{126}, \, \frac{1}{126}, \, \frac{1}{84}, \, \frac{1}{36}, \, \frac{1}{9}, \, 1 \right]\]

 \textbf{Step 3: Find the Total Score and Solve} Now we can easily calculate our two target scores:

 
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item \textbf{Score of our Starting Pad (Pad 1):} This is just the value of the very first step: $1$.\item \textbf{Score of the Winning Pad (Pad 10):} This is the total sum of all 10 steps combined. Grouping the identical matching pairs over a common denominator of 126 makes this simple to calculate:
            \end{enumerate}
            
 \[\text{Total Score of Pad 10} = 2 \left(1 + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{36} + \frac{1}{84} + \frac{1}{126}\right) = 2 \left(\frac{252 + 28 + 7 + 3 + 2}{126}\right) = \frac{146}{63}\]

 Finally, we divide our starting score by the winning pad's score to get the final probability:

 
 \[P = \frac{\text{Starting Score}}{\text{Winning Score}} = \frac{1}{\frac{146}{63}} = \boxed{\textbf{(C) } \frac{63}{146}}\]
