        	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10 2000}) In the year $2001$, the United States will host the International Mathematical Olympiad. Let $I, M,$ and $O$ be distinct positive integers such that the product $I\cdot M \cdot O=2001$. What is the largest possible value of the sum $I+M+O$?


 $\textbf{(A)}\ 23 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 55 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 99 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 111 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 671$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }E



\textbf{Solution 1}

The prime factorization of $2001$ is $3 \cdot 23 \cdot 29$.  

 The largest factor of $2001$ is $2001$.  The only way to write $2001$ as a product of three integers including $2001$ is $2001 \cdot 1 \cdot 1$, which is not allowed since $1$ is repeated.

 The second largest factor of $2001$ is $23 \cdot 29 = 667$.  The only way to write $2001$ as a product of three integers including $667$ is $667 \cdot 3 \cdot 1$, which is allowed.  In this case, the sum is $667 + 3 + 1 = 671$.

 The third largest factor of $2001$ is $3 \cdot 29 = 87$.  So, if $2001$ was written as a product of three integers without $2001$ or $667$, the largest of the integers would be at most $87$ and the sum must therefore be less than $671$.

 Therefore, the largest possible sum of the three factors is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 671}$.

 



\textbf{Solution 2 (Verifying the dumb Statement)}

First, we need to recognize that a number is going to be largest only if, of the $3$ \href{https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php?title=Factor}{factors}, two of them are small. If we want to make sure that this is correct, we could test with a smaller number, like $30$. It becomes much more clear that this is true, and in this situation, the value of $I + M + O$ would be $18$. Now, we use this process on $2001$ to get $667 * 3 * 1$ as our $3$ factors.Hence, we have $667 + 3 + 1 = \boxed{\text{(E) 671.}}$

 



\textbf{Solution 3}

The sum is the highest if two \href{https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php?title=Factor}{factors} are the lowest.

 So, $1 \cdot 3 \cdot 667 = 2001$ and $1+3+667=671 \Longrightarrow \boxed{\text{(E) 671}}$.

 



\textbf{Solution 4 (Answer Choices)}

We see since $2 + 0 + 0 + 1$ is divisible by $3$, we can eliminate all of the first $4$ answer choices because they are way too small and get $\boxed{\text{(E) 671}}$ as our final answer.

 $\textbf{(A) } \$2.53 \qquad\textbf{(B) } \$5.06 \qquad\textbf{(C) } \$6.24 \qquad\textbf{(D) } \$7.42 \qquad\textbf{(E) } \$8.77$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }D



\textbf{Solution 1}

Chandra spent $\$17.54 - \$12.48 = \$5.06$ more in January than December.  Since she used twice as much connect time in January as she did in December, she must have spent $\$5.06$ on connect time in December.  Therefore, the flat fee was $\$12.58 - \$5.06 = \boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ \$7.42}$

 \[x+y=12.48\]
 \[x+2y=17.54\]

 Solving this system of equations yields $(x, y) = (7.42, 5.06)$, so the fixed fee is $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ \$7.42}$.

 


	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10 2000}) Points $M$ and $N$ are the midpoints of sides $PA$ and $PB$ of $\triangle PAB$. As $P$ moves along a line that is parallel to side $AB$, how many of the four quantities listed below change?


 (a) the length of the segment $MN$


 (b) the perimeter of $\triangle PAB$


 (c) the area of $\triangle PAB$


 (d) the area of trapezoid $ABNM$


 
                    \begin{figure}[H]
                        \centering
                        \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Problem/AMC_10_10_2000_5_Problem_Figure_1.png}
                    \end{figure}
                    


 $\textbf{(A)}\ 0 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 1 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 4$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }B



\textbf{Solution}

Examine each quantity to determine if it changes or remains constant.

 (a) By the midsegment theorem, $MN=\frac{1}{2}AB$, so it remains constant.

 (b) If $P$ moves far to the left or right, the perimeter of $\triangle PAB$ increases without bound, so it changes.

 (c) The height of $\triangle PAB$ remains constant, as does its base $AB$.  Therefore, the area of $\triangle PAB$ remains constant.

 (d) The height of $ABNM$ remains constant, as do the lengths of its bases $AB$ and $MN$.  Therefore, the area of $ABNM$ remains constant.

 Thus $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 1}$ of these quantities changes.

	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10 2000}) The sides of a triangle with positive area have lengths $4$, $6$, and $x$. The sides of a second triangle with positive area have lengths $4$, $6$, and $y$. What is the smallest positive number that is \textbf{not} a possible value of $|x-y|$?  


 $\textbf{(A)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 4 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 6 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 8 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 10$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }D



\textbf{Solution}

By the triangle inequality, $x + 4 > 6$, so $x > 2$. Also, by the triangle inequality, $4 + 6 > x$, so $x < 10$.  Thus $2 < x < 10$, and by the same logic $2 < y < 10$.  

 So $x$ and $y$ are strictly between $2$ and $10$.  The length of this interval, which is also the smallest value which cannot be equal to $|x-y|$, is $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ 8}$.  For $0 \leq d < 8$, $|x-y|=d$ can be achieved by setting $(x, y) = \left(6+\frac{d}{2}, 6-\frac{d}{2}\right)$.

	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10 2000}) Two different prime numbers between $4$ and $18$ are chosen. When their sum is subtracted from their product, which of the following numbers could be obtained?


 $\textbf{(A)}\ 21 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 60 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 119 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 180 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 231$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }C



\textbf{Solution 1 (Meta-solving)}

Both prime numbers are odd, so their product is odd and their sum is even.  Therefore, the obtained number is odd, and (B) and (D) are incorrect. 

 The largest two prime numbers between $4$ and $18$ are $13$ and $17$, and the obtained number must be less than the product of the two primes.  So any number obtained is less than $13 \cdot 17 = 221$, so (E) is too large and therefore incorrect.

 If the prime numbers chosen are $a$ and $b$, then the required difference is $(a - 1)(b - 1) - 1$ so the minimum value is $4 \cdot 6 - 1 = 23$ so (A) is incorrect.

 This leaves $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 119}$, which is correct as $11 \cdot 13 - (11+13) = 119$.

 



\textbf{Solution 2}

Let $p$ and $q$ be the two primes.  

 Then the obtained number is $pq-(p+q)=pq-p-q=pq-p-q+1-1=(p-1)(q-1)-1$ by Simon's favorite factoring trick. 

 Since $p$ and $q$ must be odd, $(p-1)(q-1)-1$ must be odd, so (A), (B), and (D) are incorrect.

 If $(p-1)(q-1)-1 = 231$, then $(p-1)(q-1) = 232 = 2^3 \cdot 29$.  So either $p-1$ or $q-1$ must be divisible by $29$, and thus either $p$ or $q$ is at least $30$, which is too large.  So (E) is incorrect.

 This leaves $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 119}$, which is obtained if $(p, q) = (11, 13)$.

The trick is to realize that the sum $AMC+AM+MC+CA$ is similar to the product $(A+1)(M+1)(C+1)$. If we multiply $(A+1)(M+1)(C+1)$, we get 
 \[(A+1)(M+1)(C+1) = AMC + AM + AC + MC + A + M + C + 1.\] We know that $A+M+C=10$, therefore $(A+1)(M+1)(C+1) = (AMC + AM + MC + CA) + 11$ and

Then this triple $(A,M,C)$ is not optimal. (To see this, WLOG let $A\geq C+2.$ We can then increase the value of $(A+1)(M+1)(C+1)$ by changing $A \to A-1$ and $C \to C+1$. \textbf{Solution 4 (Lagrange Multipliers / Calculus)}

This problem can be modeled as an optimization problem and solved using multivariable calculus.

 Our objective function is defined as:
 \[f(A, M, C) = AMC + AM + MC + AC\]

 Subject to the constraint:
 \[g(A, M, C) = A + M + C - 10 = 0\]

 We set up the Lagrangian function $\mathcal{L}(A, M, C, \lambda)$ by subtracting the product of the multiplier $\lambda$ and the constraint function from the objective function:

 
 \[\mathcal{L}(A, M, C, \lambda) = f(A, M, C) - \lambda \cdot g(A, M, C)\]
 \[\mathcal{L}(A, M, C, \lambda) = AMC + AM + MC + AC - \lambda(A + M + C - 10)\]
 \[\mathcal{L}(A, M, C, \lambda) = AMC + AM + MC + AC - \lambda A - \lambda M - \lambda C + 10\lambda\]

 The critical points of this system are found by setting the gradient $\nabla\mathcal{L}$ equal to the zero vector. To compute the partial derivatives, we differentiate with respect to one variable while treating all other variables as constants. Setting each partial derivative to $0$ gives us the following system of equations:

 
 \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A} = MC + M + C - \lambda = 0 \implies \lambda = MC + M + C \quad \text{(Eq. 1)}\]
 \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial M} = AC + A + C - \lambda = 0 \implies \lambda = AC + A + C \quad \text{(Eq. 2)}\]
 \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial C} = AM + A + M - \lambda = 0 \implies \lambda = AM + A + M \quad \text{(Eq. 3)}\]
 \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = -(A + M + C - 10) = 0 \implies A + M + C = 10 \quad \text{(Eq. 4)}\]

 By equating $\text{Eq. 1}$ and $\text{Eq. 2}$, we get:
 \[MC + M + C = AC + A + C\]
 \[MC + M = AC + A\]
 \[MC + M - AC - A = 0\]

 Grouping terms and factoring gives:
 \[(M - A)(C + 1) = 0\]

 This equation holds if $M = A$ or $C = -1$. Since $A, M,$ and $C$ are given to be non-negative integers, $C$ cannot be $-1$, meaning only the former condition ($M = A$) can hold.

 By symmetry, equating $\text{Eq. 2}$ and $\text{Eq. 3}$ yields $A = C$. Thus, the critical point occurs when all three variables are equal:
 \[A = M = C\]

 Substituting this into the original constraint ($\text{Eq. 4}$):
 \[3A = 10 \implies A = M = C = \frac{10}{3}\]

 In the real domain, the function reaches its absolute maximum at $\left(\frac{10}{3}, \frac{10}{3}, \frac{10}{3}\right)$. Since $A, M,$ and $C$ must be integers, we look for the closest points of integral coordinates that satisfy the constraint. This is achieved by distributing the values as evenly as possible: $3, 3,$ and $4$ (in any order).

 Plugging these integer values into our objective function:
 \[f(3, 3, 4) = (3)(3)(4) + (3)(3) + (3)(4) + (3)(4) = 36 + 9 + 12 + 12 = 69\]

 Thus, the maximum value is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 69.}$.

 $\textbf{(A)}\ -\frac{1}{3} \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ -\frac{1}{9} \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 0 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{5}{9} \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{5}{3}$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }B



\textbf{Solution 1}

Let $y = \frac{x}{3}$; then $f(y) = (3y)^2 + 3y + 1 = 9y^2 + 3y+1$. Thus $f(3z)-7=81z^2+9z-6=3(9z-2)(3z+1)=0$, and $z = -\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{9}$. These sum up to $\boxed{\textbf{(B) }-\frac19}$.

 



\textbf{Solution 2 (Similar)}

This is quite trivially solved, as $3x = \dfrac{9x}{3}$, so $P(3x) = P(9x/3) = 81x^2 + 9x + 1 = 7$. $81x^2+9x-6 = 0$ has solutions $-\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{9}$. Adding these yields a solution of $\boxed{\textbf{(B) }-\frac19}$.

 



\textbf{Solution 3}

Similar to Solution 1, we have $=81z^2+9z-6=0.$ The answer is the sum of the roots, which by \href{https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php?title=Vieta\%27s\_Formulas}{Vieta's Formulas} is  $-\frac{b}{a}=-\frac{9}{81}=\boxed{\textbf{(B) }-\frac19}$.

 



\textbf{Solution 4}

Set $f\left(\frac{x}{3} \right) = x^2+x+1=7$ to get $x^2+x-6=0.$ From either finding the roots (-3 and 2), or using Vieta's formulas, we find the sum of these roots to be $-1.$ Each root of this equation is $9$ times greater than a corresponding root of $f(3z) = 7$ (because $\frac{x}{3} = 3z$ gives $x = 9z$), thus the sum of the roots in the equation $f(3z)=7$ is $-\frac{1}{9}$ or $\boxed{\textbf{(B) }-\frac19}$.

 



\textbf{Solution 5}

Since we have $f(x/3)$, $f(3z)$ occurs at $x=9z.$ Thus, $f(9z/3) = f(3z) = (9z)^2 + 9z + 1$. We set this equal to 7:

 $81z^2 + 9z +1 = 7 \Longrightarrow 81z^2 + 9z - 6 = 0$. For any quadratic $ax^2 + bx +c = 0$, the sum of the roots is $-\frac{b}{a}$. Thus, the sum of the roots of this equation is $-\frac{9}{81} = \boxed{\textbf{(B) }-\frac19}$.

 


	\item (\textit{AMC 10 10 2000}) In year $N$, the $300^\text{th}$ day of the year is a Tuesday. In year $N+1$, the $200^\text{th}$ day is also a Tuesday. On what day of the week did the $100^\text{th}$ day of year $N-1$ occur?


 $\textbf{(A)}\ \text{Thursday} \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \text{Friday} \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \text{Saturday} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \text{Sunday} \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \text{Monday}$


 
 \textbf{Answer: }A



\textbf{Solution 1}

There are either 
 \[65 + 200 = 265\] or 
 \[66 + 200 = 266\] days between the first two dates depending upon whether or not year $N+1$ is a leap year (since the February 29th of the leap year would come between the 300th day of year $N$ and 200th day of year $N + 1$). Since $7$ divides into $266$ but not $265$, for both days to be a Tuesday, year $N$ must be a leap year.

 Hence, year $N-1$ is not a leap year, and so since there are 
 \[265 + 300 = 565\] days between the date in years $N,\text{ }N-1$, this leaves a remainder of $5$ upon division by $7$. Since we are subtracting days, we count 5 days before Tuesday, which gives us  $\boxed{\mathbf{(A)} \ \text{Thursday}.}$

 



\textbf{Solution 2}

The $300-29\cdot 7=97^{\text{th}}$ day of year $N$ and the $200-15\cdot 7=95^{\text{th}}$ day of year $N+1$ are Tuesdays. If there were the same number of days in year $N$ and year $N-1,$ the $99^{\text{th}}$ day of year $N-1$ will be a Tuesday. But year $N$ is a leap year because $97-95 \cong 366 \pmod{7},$ so the $98^{\text{th}}$ day of year $N-1$ is a Tuesday. It follows that the $100^{\text{th}}$ day of year $N-1$ is $\boxed{\mathbf{(A)} \ \text{Thursday}.}$

 


\end{enumerate}
